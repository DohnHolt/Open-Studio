Sometimes an extension of care and compassion can get turned on its head and reveal conflict. Often the party braving compassion doesn't realise that there was a conflict waiting for space to open up so it could reveal itself, waiting for a tear in the seam of reality that the invitation for an awareness reveals, and sometimes what were at first thought to be one sides concerns are revealed to be just one position that makes up two sides of the conflict coin.

But sometimes the opening up of a space for conflict brings into conversation concerns that may be centred around a common situation but in fact the positions that people are emotional about and talking from might not be as entangled as at first seems. When seeking in community to operate from a we/us, to traverse conflict not from an either/or binary position but to recognise that multiple (or even all) needs can be met with one solution we first have to ask at what level are the needs operating? Is this discussion framed in such a way that all needs can even surface in this crack in reality that we call conflict, or are some needs sitting atop of others obscuring them from view, blocking them from rising to the surface of this small chasm of concern.

Concern: *When we are playing football can you not leave your bags around as I am concerned that I might hit them and break something in them.*

Retort: *Sometimes when I eat on the bench and you come along and play football and ask me to leave I find that frustrating!

On the face of it, both seem reasonable requests. However, I often remind my daughter that words are powerful and it is important not to use them to deliberately wield power, especially by omitting important truths. 

*He fell over* is the second half of the sentence and much more pleasant for her to say and for me to hear than, *I pushed him **and** he fell over*. 

Here, in our Thursday afternoon meeting where we discuss whole community problems, the bench in question is one of the goals that make up the makeshift football pitch, the other being a slide hanging off the edge of a raised yurt platform. What was interesting to me when the retort was first uttered was the casual framing of the object as bench and the obvious right to eat on the bench because, after all, it is a bench, and that is what benches are for. 

What is important to know at this point is two things. That the younger children in our community have been playing a lot of football on this pitch that they have made up and the bench has been spending 90% of it's time in use over the last three months as a goal, and that the older children were the ones who wanted to lay claim to it as a bench and eat, sat at it, when their friends are on the swings nearby. 

Here there is enough information to reconsider how the retort is framed. The certainty that it is a bench for eating, at least in this community, at this time right now, could very much be called into question. One could say this object is a *bench/goal*, however, that is not how the conversation proceeded; from the point of the retort onwards it was taken at face value that the object was indeed a bench and the solution we were working towards to resolve this conflict by dissolving the binary either/or of conflict and finding a solution that met everyone's needs was that the need for the young people was to have something that functioned as a goal and the older children to have the bench back as a bench. 

The solution was predetermined in its essence by the presupposed solidity of the definitions. 

In framing the object as a bench we were deciding that the solution would involve the younger children giving the bench back to the community. Or in other words we had already decided that the younger children's imaginations had hijacked an adult concept and that they should therefore relinquish it as part of the solution was a given. Our decision making processes are based on consensus, but by proceeding from the starting point of saying that the solution could meet both parties needs but not recognising the needs of the younger people to be validated in their experience we were in effect dismissing their experiences in the process of claiming to be meeting their needs. 

To discount the possibility that it is a goal as much as a bench is to discount the imagination that created that conception, to not allow that conception to be valid is to discount the mode of being that lives in that place of imagination, the world of an eight year old, and that, put simply, is childism. 

This last realisation came to me after the meeting but the gestalt of it was clear to me from the offset. Here we are sat in a meeting trying to operate relationally, trying to create a space where the community can come together and see that conflict is not a binary either/or zero-sum game but one in which everyone can find satisfaction. But to do so we need to operate from a we/us. How can we both have our needs met? 

I'm not saying that you can't use a bench to eat at if someone has been playing football near it, but as the words came out of their mouth, *Sometimes when I eat at the bench*... I transported the bench into my garden and imagined my three year old son had been using it for football constantly for weeks and then my daughter came down and decided to sit on it to eat. I knew that if a stand off had occured in our garden I would ask her: why of all the places you could sit and eat did you decide to sit there? What attention have you been placing on the ways other people who share this space are meeting their needs? Have you noticed what learning is going on in this space by the people all around you? These presuppositions, these noticings are the noticings of the relational human being. To wander into a space, particularly as an older person with more power and not need to pay attention to the choices that you are making and how they interact with others is a choice. 

During this meeting the younger children were having to justify their use of this bench on the terms that the older children had framed it and there is an inherent power dynamic in that. But further to that dynamic their justifications for the use of the object was not able to rest on how they viewed the space that they were the primary users of but how we the adults viewed the space as that is how the conversation was allowed to unfold.

To be clear there was not much adult involvement in this discussion, though it was held by an adult, but the bench had been put there by adults for the purpose of sitting at and the older children, our tweens and teens, were tapping into that framework, that way of seeing the world and in the process discounting the imagination of an eight year old who, when asked later how they saw the object, told me it is a goal.

In that meeting with the gestalt at the back of my mind tickling the nape of my neck but the specifics not yet on the tip of my tongue I raised an objection: it seems that the younger children are having to justify playing football, but the older children are not having to justify eating at the bench. I was still one wait at a bus stop on the way home from having a better way of framing what I was concerned about, of framing this notion that the power dynamics of three or four twelve year olds leaning on an adult concept to ask for the right to use the bench that is primarily used imaginatively by eight year olds is one that asks ultimately: whose experience of the world is most justified.

After I reflected this back to the other mentors and named it as childism, I also asked the question: *How often does this happen do you think? It just seemed so obvious to me in the moment but I am sure there are other times when I, or we, have just missed it, surely?* We discussed it further. Those of us working with young people in rights respecting settings know that "despite differences in size, experience and power, adults and children are inherently of equal worth, and that kids' perspectives and experiences should thus be considered on the same merits as those of adults." (footnote 1) We know that childism manifests itself as disempowerement of the young as adults don't take into consideration these different perspectives, ways of being and seeing the world
