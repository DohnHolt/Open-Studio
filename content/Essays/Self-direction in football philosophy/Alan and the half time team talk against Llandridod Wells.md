It was a cold, dark March Wednesday evening. The kind where the breath mists in front of your face. When you are playing football you are too busy in the moment to notice too much of what's around you. The fans, the rain, or even the bitter cold, the kind where when you stop playing you realise your fingers have curled up into balls that need time to decalcify  but sat in the tight unheated changing room, I could just make out the faces of my teammates behind the clouds being panted out. 

"Do you need me in here, or can you just sort this out yourselves?"

This was his catchphrase. In part an in-joke, something said in jest once that morphed into a methodology for managing our under-18 team at half time; in part Sir Alex Ferguson style man management to get the best out of us without us realising what he was doing (by deliberately not doing). We were top of the league, it had obviously been working. We had more often than not blown most teams away by half-time, but on those occasions when we were losing this became the pattern of the dressing room. He would come in, ask if we knew how we were going to sort it out and let us talk together and just sit in the corner and watch and listen. Maybe say a few words to emphasise a point someone made just before we went out and off we would go and win the second half. 

We hadn't lost a game until this point. We were top of the league and only needed one more victory to secure the title. However, on this March night we were playing the final game of the season against second in the league, in a whoever wins one more game wins the title decider. And we were 3-0 down. He asked us in his always unassuming manner if we needed him and we told him of course we would win and then, on the biggest game of the season, he just quietly walked out of the changing room and left it to us. Half an hour later we scored our third goal of the game to draw level, and then two minutes later we were winning 5-3 and the league was ours. 

I'd been playing football since I could walk and that was the first time in all that time that I had won a trophy. That was also my last competitive game in youth football. Being September born the following year I became an adult before the season started and men's football beckoned. Having had a starring role in that league title winning season I went on to break into the first team within a few months and graduated into semi-professional football. At approximately the same time, about a thousand miles away some little known diminutive Argentinian child first donned the stripes of Barcelona's first team, and the rest, as they say is history. 

That season Messi joined the likes of Xavi and Iniesta and Victor Valdes in the first team squad, also products of Barelona's famous La Masia academy, and within a few years they were joined by Busquets, Pique and Pedro and the bones of most would agree, the greatest club football team of all time, were there. A few years later they won their second Champions League final in three years playing breathtaking football, but it was in the years before that the seeds of that style, Juego de Posición, that has gone on to revolutionise football coaching philosophy and methodology, was being planted with these teenagers on the green grass of La Masia. 

Juego de Posición in English translates to positionism. It is the belief that the best way to win football matches is through the rational allocation and management of space. The ball does the moving not the players, who instead relate themselves and orientate themselves primarily to each other, maintaining strict distances between each other and from that rigid structures that optimise space develop. Decision making is made easier when on the ball because the structures and patterns are pre-programmed and ingrained. In it's most disciplined form it is called automatism. 

In effect Juego de Posición tries to make football, in some senses, a simple game. Zidane himself said "to play simple is to play well."

Roy Keane himself said that Brian Clough asked him pass and move quote. 

As that Barcelona team tore around Europe picking apart the best teams in the world, often far too comfortably, they really did make it look simple. But as Johan Cruyff once said, "playing football is very simple, but playing simple football is the hardest thing there is." Pep Guardiola, who oversaw the rise of this team, first as a reserve team coach with a few of the players who would later become first team regulars, then as the manager who assembled the greatest team and developed and shared tiki-taka with the world, emphasised the importance of simplicity: "Keep it simple, do the basics right, and you will be miles ahead of others.” 

Messi, the greatest player of all time, miles ahead of almost all others, says that "I just try to play and enjoy." 

Xavi Hernandez, the midfield maestro and later manager of Barcelona once said that, “In football, the result is an impostor. You can do things really, really well but not win. There’s something greater than the result, more lasting — a legacy.” 

That Barcelona team certainly created a legacy that will last for generations, however, that legacy can be traced through the history of Barcelona itself. In many ways it was simply a culmination of a certain philosophy that has been at the heart of Barcelona for decades starting with Johan Cruyff who you could argue is the most influential figure in the history of football; the most transformative in terms of his impact not just as one of the world's greatest players, but as a manager who revolutionised football philosophy.

As a player Cruyff was the embodiment and pinnacle of the Dutch style of Total Football in that great national team.  But as a coach at Barcelona Cruyff established a possession-based style of play, rooted in Total Football, that later evolved into tiki-taka. His philosophy emphasized passing, control, and maintaining possession, while fostering creativity and collective responsibility on the field. He coached Guardiola who took this philosophy to the next level, led the greatest club team in the world to multiple Champions League and La Liga titles, and later took this style to Germany and England, changing the football landscape globally as team's raced to copy the Guardiola way. When Barcelona fell on financial hardship recently they turned to a coach that was rooted in both the style and the concept of promoting youth team players who have grown up in that philosophy into the first team, Xavi Hernandez, the once captain of Guardiola's Barcelona team. 

Player to coach, to player to coach, to player to coach. This is not just a philosophy, not just a legacy, this is a lineage.

I was born just a few months after Lionel Messi. Within a year two pivotal events in the development of youth football occurred. 

In May 1988 Cruyff became manager of Barcelona and as well as assembling a team full of stars such as Koeman, Romari and Stoichkovich, he also prioritised youth development and established Barcelona’s La Masia academy as a pipeline for the first team; in doing so Cruyff embedded a long-lasting philosophy that has defined Barcelona’s identity. It is in this environment that Guardiola, and later Messi and Xavi were groomed in the art of the Barcelona way of playing.

My football journey was obviously much less stratospheric than Messi's. I remember starting my football journey in Herefordshire training in an old beat up gymnasium earmarked to be imminently knocked down. Games were played on the edge of town on the thick mud of a floodplain. My first coach was the father of one of my teammate's, a local semi-professional player, an old-school midfielder in the vein of Vinnie Jones. He made a career out of pass and move, but also his physical presence, aggression and tenacity. 

Training was enjoyable, but the coaching was of its time. I remember doing an inordinate amount of throw-in drills, the repetition of the basics was a central part of training. Improving your touch was obviously important and passing drills were conducted in pairs across the width of the gym. Pass and move was not yet in for us as eight year olds. Pass and stay by your cone, pass and stay by your cone, pass and stay by your cone. Focusing on these basic skills was the order of the day. As Guardiola says, "keep it simple, do the basics right, and you will be miles ahead of everyone else."

However, keeping the game simple is an easy yet perverse dictat. What is simple after all? For as Cruyff notes "playing football is very simple, but playing simple football is the hardest thing there is."

When I was 13 I, like Messi, made a vast jump in my footballing career. For Messi this was when he moved to Barcelona a decision promoted by their offer to pay for the medical treatments he needed to help with his groth hormone deficiency. For myself, I was what some would describe as a late developer. At this age I suddenly found my ability sky rocket, not to the level where I was playing in the academy of one of the best clubs in the world, but suddenly I was the best player in my year at school and representing the county. 

My coach when I was a teenager was probably an even better player, a youth team player for Arsenal who sadly had to retire on the eve of his professional career when he broke his leg. He, like Cruyff, believed in football being a simple game. "The ball is gold dust, you wouldn't give gold dust away would you," he told us every Sunday morning. Understanding that in theory as you sat in the dressing room as slipping on your Adidas predators was one thing, but playing football and not giving the ball away, treating it like gold dust, was for us at least, the hardest thing there was. Training was in my memory not much different. The basics were trained into us in simple drills divorced from the realities of playing football. Pass and stay by your cone, practice ten throw-ins to your partner then swap over, over and over again. Perhaps a small game at the end as reward for the drudgery of the drills. Most of my understanding of how to play football, how to make decisions in a match, I believe, came from the countless hours of playing on the playground at school, in game-like situations.

When I was 13 we played a match away on a wet, cold January morning. Our striker, through on goal in the last minute, took it around the keeper and proceeded to miss from six yards as the ball got stuck in the deep clay about a yard from the goal line. Two attempts to kick it out of that muddy hole failed before the keeper got back and dived on the ball. We lost the game by a single goal. It was around this time that Messi landed in Barcelona and had his first training sessions on the carpeted lawn of the La Masia training academy. 

Here, at La Masia, there was a pedagogy for playing simple football. There was an understanding that, as Cruyff noted, playing simple football was hard and that it therefore required an intense education, numerous lessons in an applied philosophy, an induction into a lineage. It was here that tiki-taka was first formulated, and the hours of time spent on the training ground honed the skills for a generation of talent to become so well versed in it that they would change football history forever. 

What Johan Cruyff realised was that if you're football philosophy emphasised passing, control, and maintaining possession you had to train people for that. Yes, the basics of passing and control can be taught by passing to a teammate both stood on cones on opposite sides of a run-down sports hall, but if those are subservient to the broader aim of maintaining possession, those drills aren't enough. In fact, counter-intuitively they might be making you worse. The skills that are actually required are higher level skills: creativity and innovation, game intelligence, situational awareness, managing pressure, staying calm. 

Lower level skills, what you might call in football terms technical skills, are very much needed in order to serve those more meta-cognitive skills, they are the essential bedrock upon which greatness is built, but the best way to build them up is not necessarily in a linear progression from lower to higher. In fact that best way to build them up might be simultaneously. Players were taught to keep possession and stay aware of spatial relationships—qualities that would come to define the Barcelona way of playing; they were are trained to think one step ahead, and make quick decisions and they were taught all of this through game based scenarios. 

The second important event in the history of football happened just south of Paris. A few months before the greatest ever Dutch footballer landed in Catalonia, perhaps the greatest ever French player, Michel Platini, was cutting the rope to open the first national football academy. Clairefontaine was the vision of Fernard Sastre, then-president of the French Football Federation (FFF), who envisioned it as part of a larger initiative to elevate the quality of French football, focusing on developing young talent with a centralized, highly structured training environment.

Glenn Hoddle, one of England's most technically gifted players in the 1980's, speaking on the Gary Neville podcast recently when asked was he ahead of his time agreed that maybe he was. The game back then was played in the air, on pitches that were muddy and hard to play on, and people could kick lumps out of you. To be a player who focused primarily on prizing their technical ability over physicality was an abberation, an oddness. Clairefontaine itself was ahead of its time. Sourcing the best talent from across France the idea was to provide a holistic football education. Here french teenagers learnt to value technical skills over physicality at early stages to encourage skillful play was somewhat revolutionary. 

But it was the emphasis on tactical intelligence and decision making that was almost unknown in youth coaching methodology at the time. 
Players learn to think critically about positioning, reading the game, and responding dynamically to on-field situations. The academy encouraged players to express their unique style and creativity. Rather than rigidly adhering to set patterns, Clairefontaine valued individual flair and innovation, allowing players to develop their own approaches within the team’s structure. 

Clairefontaine focused on developing both the technical skills and game understanding through small sided games and game-based scenarios, which form a core part of Clairefontaine’s curriculum. Instead of isolated drills, players trained in dynamic situations that replicate real match contexts. These scenarios were designed to challenge players to use their skills thoughtfully, encouraging them to solve problems on the pitch as they would in a game. By focusing on realistic match conditions, Clairefontaine’s approach fostered players who were technically adept, tactically aware, and mentally sharp, which aligned with the academy's emphasis on developing well-rounded, adaptable footballers. 

In 1998, France hosted the World Cup, and beat Brazil 3-0 to lift the Jules Rimet for the first time. The first generation of Clairefontaine graduates were starting to break through into the national team. Fabien Barthez and Lillian Thuram both started that final.

Two years later France became the first country to hold both the World Cup and the Euros simultaneously. In the final Italy took the lead early on and held on until the last minute of the game when Sylvan Wiltord scored taking the game to extra-time. Then in extra-time Robert Pires pulled the ball back from the byline and David Trezeguet rifled the ball into the top corner to win the game with a golden goal. All three of those players had their football education firmly in Clairefontaine. The most famous product of the academy, Thierry Henry scored three goals that tournament and was man of the match in the final. 

Fernard Sastre was rightly vindicated. Clairefontaine had gone on to produce the global superstars that could bring home national glory. And across the Pyreness the Royal Spanish Football Federation (RFEF) were already taking notes. 

They didn't have to look too far to replicate this French experiment for within their borders in the north east was an academy that was treating young footballers with the same philosophy and methodology. La Masia and Clairefontaine held similar values in developing modern footballers. Both subscribed to a holisitic pedagogy that emphasised academic instruction and character development as well as technical football skills. But most importantly, and revolutionary, was their emphasis on creating the conditions for players to own and hone their decision making, creativity and football intelligence and the recognition that these are best honed through game based scenarios rather than repetitive drills. 

Instead of an emphasis on creating a strong national academy, however, the RFEF decided to implement a joined up national strategy that encouraged a uniform playing style, focusing on technical skills, quick passing and tactical awareness. Coaches across Spain were encouraged to adopt this philosophy, creating a cohesive identity across club academies. Leaning on the La Masia philosophy and indeed its reputation this approach was quickly embraced nationally and in 2003 La Ciudad del Futbol was opened. However, this is not a national academy in the mould of Clairefontaine. The RFEF maintain this central hub but still adhere to a decentralised vision of creating the next generation of potential World Cup winners within the club's academies through a coordinated national strategy.

Within a similar time frame to France this work bore the desired fruits. Around a decade after implementation Spain won their second Euro title. The tiki-taka style of Barcelona transitioned seamlessly into the national team as Vincent Bosque built his team around the great Guardiola team with the addition of similarly gifted technical players such as Fabregas (who was at La Masia until 17) and Xabi Alonso. Two years later, sandwiched between Barcelona's two Champions League victories, they, like France, went on to win their first ever World Cup. Two years later they did what no team has ever done and won three major international titles in a row when they retained their UEFA European Championship. 

Two years later they were surprisingly knocked out of the group stages and Germany went on to win the World Cup for the fourth time, their first as a unified country. Like France and Spain before this success came off the back of a similar push a decade previously to revamp the coaching philosophy and strategy at youth level. In a similar vein to Spain Germany took a decentralised but co-ordinated approach, with 360 regional training centres, and a requirement on clubs in the top two divisions to have academies that met a minimum required standard. The German Football Association (DFB) emphasised a transition in coaching methodology towards developing players that were technically skilled, like in Spain, but also leant more towards physical development whilst keeping the core curriculum component of both France and Spain of developing the cognitive components of elite football but with an emphasis on tactical versatility, creating players who were both astute and flexible. Again all this done through game based scenarios, which presented a large shift in their training approach. 

These were the generations that spanned my adult football career. Having started playing semi-professional football in the mid-2000's I spent most of my career playing football on a Saturday afternoon, running around central midfield trying to emulate what I had seen Cesc Fabregas, Arsenal's midfield maestro, doing on Match of the Day the previous weekend. And as he picked up his third major international medal at the 2012 Euros I retired from football at the unripe age of 25. My journey from that run down gymnasium, through the muddy fields of Herefordshire, to playing across the whole Birmingham area, ended with one season in Manchester playing for the University first team, before I decided to call it a day. Around the time that that great French player, Michel Platini, hung a medal around Fabregas' neck, and I hung up my boots, some guy working in admin in Staffordshire took a pair of scissors off of Prince William and hung them up on a nail in a small room as he stayed around shook the hands of plenty of dignitaries at St George's Park, the English FA's new national football centre. Twenty four years after France, over ten years later than Spain and Germany, behind others like Belgium, the Netherlands, and Italy, England finally decided to revamp their youth football structure. No longer would children have to endure twenty minutes of throw-in practice, or pass and stay on your cone. The future, finally, was here.
